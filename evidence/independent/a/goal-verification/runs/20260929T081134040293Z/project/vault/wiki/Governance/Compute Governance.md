# Compute Governance

Behavioral red lines are difficult to enforce without a better understanding of system internals. Compute governance is considered more tractable by regulating training runs and hardware rather than software, as software can be freely copied and transmitted, and malware costs approximately \$12T/year globally and is largely uncontrolled. Hardware governance could involve requiring safety checks embedded by chip manufacturers like TSMC, which would currently require building a fab from scratch.

Treaty organization models compared include the ICAO/IMO model, which regulates national regulators with a hands-off approach, and the IAEA model, which allows direct inspection powers and facility entry but is politically difficult due to jurisdictional issues (e.g., US opposition). The CTBTO experience suggests UN structural impediments (e.g., 5-continent division requirement, 4-year term limits, lack of institutional memory) make building competent organizations challenging, suggesting an alternative might be UN-affiliated but independently run.

## Useful details
* Compute governance focuses on regulating training runs and hardware.
* Software governance is limited by the ease of copying and transmitting software.
* Hardware governance could involve requiring safety checks embedded by chip manufacturers.
* Treaty models contrast a hands-off national regulator approach (ICAO/IMO) with a direct inspection approach (IAEA).
* UN structural impediments are cited as a barrier to building competent organizations.
