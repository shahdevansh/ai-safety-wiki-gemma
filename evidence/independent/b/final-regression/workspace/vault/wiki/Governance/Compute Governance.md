# Compute Governance

Behavioral red lines are difficult to enforce without a better understanding of system internals. Compute governance is considered more tractable by regulating training runs and hardware rather than software, as software can be freely copied and transmitted, and malware costs approximately \$12T/year globally and is largely uncontrolled. Hardware governance could involve requiring safety checks embedded by chip manufacturers; bypassing this would currently require building a fabrication facility from scratch, which is deemed infeasible.

Treaty organization models include the ICAO/IMO model, which regulates national regulators with a hands-off approach, and the IAEA model, which allows direct inspection powers and facility entry but is politically difficult due to jurisdictional issues (e.g., US opposition). The CTBTO experience suggests UN structural impediments (e.g., 5-continent division requirement, 4-year term limits, lack of institutional memory) make building competent organizations challenging, suggesting an alternative like a UN-affiliated but independently run body might be preferable.
